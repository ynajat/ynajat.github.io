Ethics of Web Scraping: OkCupid

Author

Yousif Najat

Published

November 24, 2026

Introduction

In 2016, a group of Danish researchers web-scraped and publicly released approximately 70,000 dating profiles from OkCupid, a popular online dating site, without the consent of either the users or OkCupid (Resnick 2016). Arguing that the information was publicly available, the researchers published highly personal information about users, including their age, gender, religious affiliation, personality traits, sexual habits, and political beliefs, without making any meaningful effort to anonymize the data (Resnick 2016). Although the researchers did not release users’ real names, the combination of information they published made it possible to identify individual users. This incident sparked great controversy within the data science community, raising important ethical questions about web scraping and the distinction between publicly available data and data that is ethically permissible to collect and distribute. More specifically, this incident raises the question of whether it is ethical to scrape and collect publicly available data when doing so comes at the expense of users’ privacy.

In the sections below, we will dive deeper into this incident and the ethical questions it raises.

Questions about Consent: What was the consent structure for users? Were the users aware of the ways their data would be used for research? Was informed consent possible?

A major component of ethics within data science is protecting user privacy and obtaining informed consent from users, where informed consent means that users understand that their data is being collected and how it will be used (Qualitative Data Repository, n.d.). In fact, the Data Values and Principles Manifesto (Data Practices 2018) lists “[p]rotect the privacy and security of individuals represented in our data” as one of its major principles. With these considerations in mind, it becomes clear to see that the OkCupid incident in many ways fails to uphold user privacy and informed consent as the researchers not only failed to inform users that their data would be collected, but also did not inform users that their personal data would be made publicly available. Hence, users did not know about the scraping of their data and did not provide any form of informed consent to the researchers. While the researchers argue that this did not constitute a breach of user privacy or consent because the data was publicly available, OkCupid and the general public argue that it still constitutes a breach of OkCupid’s Terms of Service and the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (Raman et al. 2023), which criminalizes unauthorized computer access and computer fraud. OkCupid and the general public do make valid arguments as the researchers could have taken steps toward obtaining informed consent, such as seeking permission from OkCupid, notifying users, or collecting the data with greater anonymization (Raman et al. 2023). Yet, did not.

Questions about Data Accessibility: Were the data made publicly available? Why? How? On what platform?

While making datasets publicly available can help create greater transparency, it can also infringe upon the privacy of the individuals whose data is being collected (Resnick 2016). Unfortunately, the OkCupid incident is an example of the latter. After running software to scrape data from OkCupid, the researchers uploaded the entirety of the 70,000 dating profiles to the Open Science Framework, an online forum where researchers share their raw data to promote transparency within their work (Resnick 2016). By doing so, the researchers exposed the personally identifiable information they had collected as part of each dating profile to the general public, with little anonymization—causing huge privacy concerns as individual users could now be identified by combining various parts of the scraped information (Resnick 2016). When questioned about this decision, the researchers responded: “‘…all the data found in the dataset are or were already publicly available, so releasing this dataset [in the forum] merely presents it [in] a more useful form’” (Resnick 2016). Therefore, from the researchers’ perspective, uploading the dataset to the forum was a way of increasing the data’s accessibility; however, doing so also came at the price of users privacy, especially as little anonymization efforts were taken.

Questions about User Identifiability: Is the data identifiable? All of it? Some of it? In what way? Are the data sufficiently anonymized or old to be free of ethical concerns? Is anonymity guaranteed?

Though user privacy can entail many things, one aspect of user privacy is the protection of the user’s identity, such that collected data cannot be directly pinpointed back to a specific user. In the context of the OkCupid incident, although no real names were provided alongside each extracted dating profile, highly detailed and personal information about each user was included, such that individual users could be identified (Resnick 2016). This information included aspects such as a user’s age, gender, religion, personality, sexual habits, political beliefs, fidelity, feelings on homosexuality, and username (Resnick 2016). Thus, by scraping and publicly releasing these variables together, the researchers made it possible for external individuals to use clues from a user’s demographic information and username to potentially identify that specific user (Resnick 2016). Hence, although individual variables may not have been enough to identify a user on their own, the dataset as a whole was highly identifiable, as a combination of personal information in the dataset could be used to narrow down an individual’s identity. Now, in terms of anonymization, the researchers made it clear that they did not make any efforts to anonymize the dataset, even after being confronted by fellow community members (Resnick 2016). Although anonymity was not guaranteed in this context, the researchers could have taken steps toward anonymization by implementing techniques like removing unnecessary variables or adding random noise to help reduce the identifiability of the data (Raman et al. 2023)—however, they also took no such action.

Questions about Unintended Uses of Data: Is the data being used in unintended ways to the original study?

Although the researchers originally chose to scrape data from OkCupid as it is a valuable source of survey information, given that users answer many survey-like questions, and hoped to use the data to help address some of their research interests (Resnick 2016), the attention that this incident caused has also highlighted a new potential use for the OkCupid dataset: as a pedagogical tool for students (Raman et al. 2023). More specifically, the dataset provides a valuable opportunity for students to not only explore the ethical issues surrounding data collection (like web scraping) but also practice various anonymization techniques (Raman et al. 2023). However, those looking to implement the dataset in classrooms and institutions still argue that further anonymization is needed before it can be properly used (Raman et al. 2023). Nevertheless, with some further anonymization, the OkCupid dataset could serve purposes beyond what the researchers had initially intended and function as a valuable learning opportunity for the future.

Why this matters?

The OkCupid incident matters because it provides insight into and brings us back to the question: Is it ethical to scrape and collect publicly available data when doing so comes at a cost of user privacy and consent? More specifically, this incident demonstrates that just because data is publicly available that does not necessarily mean that collecting and publicly distributing it is free from ethical concerns, especially when the cost is users’ privacy and consent. In fact, through this incident we directly observe the cost of substituting the public accessibility of the data for informed user consent, particularly when we consider who benefits—the few researchers—and who is harmed—the approximately 70,000 OkCupid users whose personal information was released.

Ultimately, this is what makes this incident significant: it depicts the consequences of prioritizing public accessibility over informed user consent and privacy, and challenges those who collect data—like researchers—to look beyond whether data is publicly accessible and consider whether it should be collected in the first place.
